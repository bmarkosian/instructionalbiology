How Exosomes Could Deliver Medicines Inside the Body

Instructional Biology

Many modern medicines are fragile. RNA drugs, for example, need delivery technology that is efficient, tissue-specific and non-immunogenic before they can reach their target.[3] Exosome drug delivery uses one of nature's own vehicles: the tiny, membrane-wrapped packages that cells release to send molecules to one another.

This article follows a drug-loaded exosome on its journey, from the moment it is made and injected to the moment it unloads its cargo inside a target cell, and looks at the animal studies that put exosome drug delivery on the map. For the basics of what exosomes are and how cells make them, see our explainer What Are Exosomes?.

What is exosome drug delivery?

Exosomes are a subset of extracellular vesicles, with an average diameter of about 100 nanometers. They form inside cells, in compartments called endosomes, and naturally carry nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, amino acids and metabolites that reflect the cell they came from.[1] Because cells already use them to exchange material, researchers see them as a template for therapeutics: if cells can ship molecules this way, perhaps medicines can travel the same route.[1]

Several studies suggest extracellular vesicles have advantages over conventional synthetic carriers, and a 2021 review in Nature Nanotechnology described them as a candidate next-generation drug delivery platform. The same review notes that turning them into approved therapies remains challenging.[2]

Step 1: Choosing the producer cell

An exosome's journey starts with the cell that makes it, and that choice matters. In a landmark 2011 study, researchers at the University of Oxford produced exosomes from the mice's own dendritic cells, a type of immune cell, specifically to reduce the chance of an immune reaction.[3] A 2017 study from MD Anderson Cancer Center used exosomes from normal fibroblast-like mesenchymal cells instead.[4]

The source cell also shapes where vesicles travel. When a Karolinska Institutet and Oxford team compared vesicles from three different mouse cell sources, they found differences in biodistribution related to the cells of origin.[5]

Step 2: Loading the cargo and adding an address label

Natural exosomes do not carry the drug a researcher wants, so it has to be loaded. In the 2011 Oxford study, purified exosomes were loaded with short interfering RNA (siRNA) using electroporation, a brief electrical pulse that temporarily opens the vesicle membrane.[3]

The team also gave their exosomes an address label. They engineered the producer cells to display a short peptide from the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG), which binds to nerve cells, fused to an exosome membrane protein called Lamp2b. Because Lamp2b is an exosomal membrane protein, the RVG peptide was displayed on the surface of the exosomes those cells released.[3] Loading methods, in-depth characterization and large-scale manufacturing are now treated as the critical design steps for any vesicle-based drug.[2]

Step 3: Into the bloodstream

Once injected into a vein, an exosome enters a crowded and hostile environment. A Kyoto University study that tracked exosomes from mouse melanoma cells found that they disappeared from the blood very quickly, with a half-life of about two minutes. The signal went first to the liver and then to the lungs.[6]

A follow-up study showed why. Macrophages, the immune system's clean-up cells, took up the exosomes in the liver and spleen, while endothelial cells took them up in the lungs. In mice whose macrophages had been depleted, clearance from the blood was much slower.[7]

Larger biodistribution work confirms the pattern. Injected vesicles accumulated mainly in the liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract and lungs, and the route of administration and the dose both changed where they went.[5]

Step 4: Avoiding the clean-up crew

Exosomes have a natural advantage here. The 2017 MD Anderson study found that exosomes stayed in circulation longer than liposomes, a widely used synthetic carrier, and traced this to a natural surface protein that works like a "don't eat me" signal, helping protect exosomes from being swallowed by monocytes and macrophages.[4]

Step 5: Finding the target tissue

This is where the address label proves its worth. In the 2011 Oxford study, intravenously injected RVG-targeted exosomes delivered their siRNA to neurons, microglia and oligodendrocytes in the brain, a hard-to-reach organ, and the authors did not observe non-specific uptake in the other tissues they examined.[3] In the Karolinska biodistribution study, adding the RVG targeting peptide made vesicles appear more readily in acetylcholine-receptor-rich organs, and vesicles also accumulated in tumor tissue in tumor-bearing mice.[5]

Step 6: Getting inside the cell

Reaching a cell is not the same as entering it. To change what a cell does, a vesicle's cargo has to cross a membrane, either by fusing directly with the cell's outer membrane or by fusing with an internal endosome after the cell has swallowed it.[8] Cells take up extracellular vesicles by several routes, including clathrin-dependent endocytosis, caveolin-mediated uptake, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis and lipid raft-mediated internalization. Which route is used appears to depend on proteins and glycoproteins on the surface of both the vesicle and the target cell.[8] In the 2017 cancer study, exosome uptake by tumor cells was facilitated by macropinocytosis, a form of cellular drinking.[4]

Step 7: Unloading the medicine

The final test is whether the cargo does its job. In the Oxford study, exosomes carrying siRNA against BACE1, an enzyme studied as a target in Alzheimer's disease, reduced BACE1 messenger RNA by 60% and BACE1 protein by 62% in the brains of wild-type mice. Prior exposure to the targeted exosomes did not weaken the effect.[3]

In the MD Anderson study, exosomes engineered to carry siRNA or short hairpin RNA against mutant KRAS, a key driver of pancreatic cancer, were named iExosomes. They suppressed cancer in multiple mouse models of pancreatic cancer and significantly increased overall survival, with an effect that depended on that same surface protein.[4]

What the evidence shows, and what comes next

These studies were carried out in mice. They show that exosomes can carry RNA medicines through the bloodstream, that surface engineering can steer them toward specific tissues, and that their natural surface proteins can help them avoid early clearance.[3–5] They also show the main engineering tasks ahead: rapid clearance by the liver and spleen, biodistribution that varies with cell source, dose and route, and the need for reliable loading, characterization and large-scale manufacturing.[2,5–7]

For scientists and partners, the practical lesson is that an exosome drug is defined by more than its cargo. The producer cell, the surface markers, the loading method, the dose and the route all shape where the vesicle goes and what it delivers. That makes exosome drug delivery a manufacturing and design discipline as much as a biological one.[2,5]
