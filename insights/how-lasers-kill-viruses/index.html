How Lasers Kill Viruses

Instructional Biology Inc.

Killing a virus without heat sounds like it should require some kind of exotic radiation — the kind that ionizes molecules and, as a side effect, tends to damage everything else nearby too. It turns out there's a gentler way, and it works by something closer to opera than chemistry: hitting a virus with light tuned to make it shake itself apart.

The mechanism: impulsive stimulated Raman scattering

A viral capsid — the protein shell that packages a virus's genetic material — is held together partly by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts between its protein subunits. Like any physical structure, it has natural mechanical vibration modes, the molecular-scale equivalent of a wine glass's resonant frequency. Hit a wine glass with sound at exactly its resonant frequency, with enough energy, and it shatters. Hit a viral capsid with a laser pulse tuned to excite its own resonant vibrational mode, and something structurally similar happens: the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts holding the capsid together weaken and break, and the capsid proteins aggregate into a form the virus can no longer use.

The physical process behind this is called impulsive stimulated Raman scattering, or ISRS — a well-established way of driving large-amplitude vibrations in molecules using an extremely short laser pulse, first demonstrated for virus inactivation in a 2007 paper in the Journal of Biomedical Optics by Tsen and colleagues, who showed that a low-power visible femtosecond laser could inactivate M13 bacteriophages through exactly this mechanism.

Why "ultrashort" is the whole point

The laser pulses involved are femtosecond-scale — millionths of a billionth of a second — which matters for two reasons. First, a pulse that short can deliver a sharp mechanical impulse to the capsid's vibrational mode without depositing enough sustained energy to heat the surrounding material, which is what makes this a genuinely non-thermal process rather than just a very fast version of heat sterilization. Second, published follow-up work found the effect is sensitive to both wavelength and pulse width together, not either alone — change one without the other and inactivation efficiency drops, which is a strong signal that a real mechanical resonance, not incidental heating, is doing the work.

Subsequent research extended the finding well beyond the original bacteriophage result: murine cytomegalovirus, murine norovirus, and HIV have all been shown to be susceptible to the same class of ultrashort-pulse laser treatment, generally in the visible-to-near-infrared range. One study went further and identified the functional consequence directly — laser-treated virus particles could still enter a host cell, but the aggregated capsid protein couldn't uncoat properly once inside, trapping the viral genome and blocking replication before it could start.

Why not just use UV, like everyone already does

Ultraviolet and gamma irradiation are established, effective pathogen-reduction tools, and they work by a completely different mechanism — direct photon absorption that damages nucleic acids and, along the way, ionizes and damages nearby proteins too. That collateral damage is precisely the problem for anything where the protein content is the product you're trying to preserve, not just an inert carrier for it. ISRS-based inactivation is comparatively selective: visible light is barely absorbed by nucleic acids or most proteins in the first place, so the energy goes almost entirely into the specific mechanical resonance being targeted, largely sparing the surrounding biological material.

Where this fits our platform

This is the published scientific basis for the pathogen-reduction system in our five-part technology stack — ultrashort-pulse optical clearance, tuned below the threshold that damages protein structure, operating in the same visible-to-near-infrared, femtosecond-to-sub-picosecond regime this body of research has established. We didn't invent the underlying physics; ISRS-based virus inactivation has been documented in the peer-reviewed literature since 2007. What we've built is a bioprocessing system that applies it as a non-thermal pathogen-safety layer for material where preserving native protein structure is the entire point — which is exactly the constraint our bio-processing system exists to satisfy.
