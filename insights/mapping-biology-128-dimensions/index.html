Mapping Biology In 128 Dimensions: What ASAP Learned

Instructional Biology

Biology is not flat. One signaling protein reaches a handful of receptors; each receptor sets off cascades; the cascades fan out to thousands of outcomes across the body. Any map of that branching has to make room for it, and most machine-learning maps do not. ASAP, the Advanced Science AI Platform at the center of Instructional Biology, was built on a different shape of space. This is what it is, and what it learned when it was trained.

Update, November 4, 2026: the graph is frozen. ASAP's knowledge graph is now frozen as its final record, the version all of our work runs on. It holds 12,835 nodes and 35,004 edges (28,837 of its literature edges signed), built from 19,519 PubMed papers and 11,537 clinical trials, on a trained 128-dimensional Poincaré ball with mean distortion 0.0469 on held-out pairs, within the 0.05 gate.

Its locked network turns each signed edge's message by a trained angle in each of 64 planes of the receiving node's tangent space: 33,024 × 64 trained angles. Asked about every constituent against every disease and disease phenotype (351,890 pairs), it called 20,734 predicted benefits and flagged 14,269 contraindications. 19,251 of those benefit calls are new, across 2,409 diseases and disease phenotypes (202 diseases and 2,207 phenotypes) where the record has no direct link, no shared paper and no tested trial. 20 demo patient digital twins, each a simulated subject, now run on it.

The figures below are the record of 1 November 2026, when the network was trained. All of them are computational results, not clinical results.

Why A Curved Map

In flat (Euclidean) space, the room around a point grows only polynomially as you move outward. A branching tree grows exponentially. Squeeze one into the other and the outer branches collide: distinct signals land on top of each other and distances stop meaning anything. In hyperbolic space, room grows exponentially toward the edge, which is why researchers have shown that hierarchies embed in it with far less distortion than in flat space (Nickel and Kiela, NeurIPS 2017).

ASAP places its signaling map inside a Poincaré ball, the standard model of hyperbolic space. The medicine's own proteins sit near the center; receptors, cascades and master regulators fill the middle rings; diseases and measurable outcomes sit toward the rim.

What Is In The Map

The map is the Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph (SCKG). Every link says that one thing raises or lowers another, and carries the published papers behind it.

  • 15,652 biological entities, from signaling proteins to diseases.
  • 39,301 links, of which 33,024 are signed: they state a direction, "raises" or "lowers".
  • 19,462 PubMed papers gathered, 17,766 of them cited on the map's links.
  • 11,444 clinical trials gathered as evidence.

What Training Showed

A hyperbolic graph neural network learned the map in 128 dimensions, the ceiling set in the patent application, over 13.9 hours of training. Two results matter most.

  • It keeps the shape. Mean distortion on 19,200 held-out pairs of nodes was 0.0469, within the 0.05 gate the patent application sets before a map can be promoted.
  • It learns direction. With 90% of the training links hidden, it recovered the direction of 89.0% of them (22,491 of 25,272).

200 New Places To Look

ASAP then scored each of our signaling proteins, one at a time, against every disease in the map: 28,721 protein–disease pairs. It returned 1,529 predicted benefits across 200 diseases where our evidence base has no direct link yet. It also flagged 1,208 contraindications, because it reports harm as well as benefit and never discards a warning.

These are hypotheses to test, not treatments. Each one comes with its evidence route, paper by paper, so a scientist can check why ASAP made the call before anyone runs an experiment.

Why It Matters

A medicine made of many native signals needs a map of many signals. ASAP is the engine behind every patient digital twin and every composition we design: it tells us where to look, which direction each signal pushes, and where the body is likely to push back.
