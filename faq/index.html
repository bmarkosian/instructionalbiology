About Instructional Biology

What is Instructional Biology?

+

Instructional Biology is a pre-clinical biotechnology company developing a connected technology stack — a biology-native AI reasoning system paired with a physical bio-processing protocol — aimed at reasoning across biological signaling as a connected system rather than an isolated list of targets.

What does ASAP stand for, and what does it do?

+

ASAP is our Advanced Science AI Platform. It organizes biological evidence into a structured, hierarchy-aware graph — the Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph (SCKG) — so relationships between signals, receptors, cascades, and outcomes can be reasoned about explicitly instead of inferred from a flat list.

What is the Markosian High-Fidelity Bio-Processing Protocol (MHFBP)?

+

MHFBP is a controlled, lower-temperature approach to preserving the native structure of complex biological material during processing, in contrast to legacy methods that trade structural fidelity for industrial throughput. It's the physical-processing layer our reasoning systems depend on having intact evidence to work with.

What technologies make up the platform?

+

Five connected layers: the Markosian High-Fidelity Bio-Processing Protocol, a pathogen-reduction system, a carrier (exosome) manufacturing system, the reasoning system (SCKG, reasoned over by ASAP), and a therapeutic-regulation system that operates within a clinician-authorized envelope. See Our Technologies for the full stack.

What stage is the company at?

+

Instructional Biology is a pre-clinical biotechnology company with a patent-pending platform. Our platform and programs are under active development and have not completed clinical development. Nothing on this site is a claim of clinical efficacy, regulatory approval, or medical advice — we say that plainly and repeat it often.

Is this available to patients or clinicians right now?

+

Not yet. Our public materials state exactly where the science stands, without overstating it. If you're a clinician or researcher with a specific question, we welcome it — reach out through our contact page.

Who founded Instructional Biology, and where is the company based?

+

The company was founded by Boris Markosian, building on more than a decade of prior work across neuroscience, computational data architecture, and biological operations. Instructional Biology was formally founded on February 5, 2026, and is based in Oceanside, California. Read more on the founder's story page.

What is Instructional Medicine?

+

Instructional Medicine is our therapeutic framework for a more context-aware approach to biology: identify how biological signals relate to one another, preserve what they mean, and build around what the system is communicating, rather than forcing a single receptor. It brings our five technology systems together as one integrated program, from AI discovery through manufacturing, delivery and patient-specific feedback. Read the science behind it.

What is a digital twin in medicine?

+

A digital twin in medicine is a computational model of one person's biological signaling, built from their own data, that updates as new signals arrive. It is not a duplicate of a person or a crystal ball; it is a model bounded by the evidence that built it. For Instructional Biology it is a multi-year research direction, and we do not currently offer a digital twin product. Read our digital twins explainer.

Does Instructional Biology's system make treatment decisions on its own?

+

No. In our therapeutic-regulation system, closed-loop means the loop closes only within boundaries a clinician has set in advance, a prescriber-authorized envelope. Any signal outside that envelope escalates to a human reviewer rather than triggering an autonomous change. See how the therapeutic-regulation system works.

What is the Thermosensitive Peptidome (TSP)?

+

The Thermosensitive Peptidome, or TSP, is our term for the collection of temperature-sensitive signaling peptides and proteins we seek to protect during processing. These molecules carry their function in their three-dimensional fold, which heat can undo even without breaking a chemical bond. Read the Thermosensitive Peptidome explainer.

Why does the Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph use hyperbolic space?

+

Biological signaling forms a hierarchy: a few master regulators, then exponentially more cascades, receptors, peptides, disease states and outcomes. In hyperbolic space, room grows exponentially as you move outward, matching how that hierarchy branches, so the graph fits without crowding. The full graph is embedded in a Poincaré ball of up to 128 dimensions, building on Nickel and Kiela's 2017 work on Poincaré embeddings. Open the interactive Poincaré Explorer.

Can a company license one Instructional Biology technology without the rest?

+

Yes. Our five technology families do not stand or fall together: four of them, ISRS-ML pathogen reduction, the MET-APFS (Markosian Exosomal Transcytosis - Autonomic Pharmacokinetic Feedback System) carrier, ASAP and the digital twin, do not depend on the bioprocessing claims, so a company could license any of them on its own. All five are available for discussion individually or as a whole. See the five technology families.

What are exosomes, and why are they studied for drug delivery?

+

Exosomes are nanoscale, membrane-wrapped particles released by cells, roughly 40 to 160 nanometres across, that carry proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and metabolites. Researchers study them as natural drug-delivery carriers, and our carrier (exosome) manufacturing system builds engineered delivery vesicles modeled on this transport mechanism. Read our exosome explainer.

The Science And The Business, Answered

Why is medicine moving beyond chemical reductionism?

+

Most drugs work by forcing a single target on or off. The body's signaling networks are redundant and adaptive, so they compensate, which can bring side effects and tolerance. Instructional Medicine takes a different approach: it uses the signaling proteins the body already makes, kept in their native shape, to work with those networks rather than against them. As founder Boris Markosian puts it: “Medicine is changing, we have evolved beyond chemical reductionism. The future of medicine is instructional.”

Why does a protein's three-dimensional shape matter so much?

+

A signaling protein's message is carried by its folded shape: receptors recognize the shape, not just the chain of amino acids. Heat, shear and harsh chemical processing can unfold a protein, and an unfolded protein can lose its activity or provoke an immune response. That is why every step of our platform is designed to keep proteins in their native shape. Read why heat denatures proteins.

Why use proteins that closely match the body's own?

+

Many signaling proteins are highly conserved across mammals, so they closely match their human counterparts in sequence and structure. We isolate these naturally occurring proteins and study them as multi-protein ensembles, the way the body uses them, rather than as single synthetic molecules.

What is POEF, and what is it being developed for?

+

POEF (Placental Opioid-Enhancing Factor) is our first drug candidate, a small native peptide fraction. Published animal research suggests it enhances the body's own pain-relief pathways. Our aim is pain relief that needs fewer opioids, with opioid use disorder, post-surgical pain and neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome as target areas. POEF is patent-pending and has not yet been tested in our own human studies or approved for any use. Read about VPE-3 (POEF).

How can enzyme processing be stopped without heat?

+

Many extraction processes stop enzyme digestion by heating, which also damages the proteins being collected. Our Thermodynamic Cold-Stop technology is designed to pause enzyme activity at the right moment without heat, so the proteins keep their native shape. The specific conditions are part of our pending patent application. Read about Thermodynamic Cold-Stop.

What is the Markosian Thermal Guardrail?

+

It is a control approach that watches the most heat-sensitive protein in a batch and keeps the process safely below the temperature at which that protein begins to unfold. The limit is not one fixed number: it is set for each material from measurements of that material, with a safety margin decided before the batch starts.

How can a laser clear viruses without heating the medicine?

+

Our ISRS-ML system uses ultrashort laser pulses tuned to the mechanical vibration of viral shells, much as a singer's note can shatter a wine glass. The aim is to break viruses apart while the therapeutic proteins stay intact, with sensors checking both results in real time. The system is in development. Read how lasers can clear viruses.

How does ASAP know whether a signal raises or lowers an effect?

+

Every link in ASAP's knowledge graph is signed: it records whether one entity raises or lowers another, with the published paper behind it. The model passes these signed messages along the graph, so a prediction keeps its direction and can be traced step by step to its sources. Read about knowledge graphs in biology.

How do you build a drug interaction and clinical relationship knowledge graph?

+

Start by deciding what the nodes are: drugs, proteins, genes, diseases, side effects and clinical outcomes. Pull relationships from curated databases and the published literature, keep the citation on every edge, and match identifiers so the same drug or disease isn't counted twice. Then add the clinical relationships (indications, contraindications and trial results) and drug–drug interactions, turn the graph into numbers a model can learn from, and test its predictions on treatments it hasn't seen. Our Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph (SCKG) is built on the same principles for signaling biology: it links 21,425 papers and 12,014 clinical trials, and its trained network has run 351,890 computational simulations. Read the seven steps, with their sources.

How do engineered carriers protect proteins on the way in?

+

Our engineered exosome carriers (MET-APFS) are designed to enclose native proteins, shield them from rapid breakdown and immune clearance, and guide them toward target tissues. Because the carrier works with many kinds of cargo, it can also be licensed to deliver other companies' therapies. Read about carrier manufacturing.

What stops a digital twin from giving an unsafe dose?

+

Every adjustment stays inside limits a licensed physician sets in advance. A separate safety controller in the delivery device checks each command against those limits, can always reduce or pause a dose, and falls back to a safe baseline if sensor data is lost. Any increase beyond the limits needs the physician's approval. See the patient digital twin.

Will there be non-prescription products?

+

Over-the-counter and cosmetic products are among our planned commercial streams. In that setting a digital twin would only offer suggestions based on a person's wearable data: the person decides, and no device is controlled automatically. Read about OTC products.

How will a buyer know a product is genuine?

+

Our platform is designed to confirm authenticity from markers inside the product itself, combined with a native-state quality score recorded on a tamper-evident ledger, so a sample can be checked without relying on packaging or labels.

What are Instructional Biology's ten commercial streams?

+

ASAP AI studies and licensing; ISRS-ML pathogen reduction; Thermodynamic Cold-Stop skids; bioreactor retrofits and skids; OTC products; cosmeceuticals; in-silico toxicology and clinical trials; clinical (B2B) digital twins; consumer (B2C) wearables; and therapeutics with our Vanguard programs.
