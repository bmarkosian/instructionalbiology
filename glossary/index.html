Instructional Biology Inc. — Glossary

Glossary

Instructional Biology works where biology, manufacturing and AI meet, and each field brings its own vocabulary. This glossary explains the terms used across www.instructionalbiology.com in plain language: our own names, such as Instructional Medicine, ASAP and MHFBP, alongside the general science terms they build on, such as peptides, exosomes and hyperbolic space. Each entry links to the page that explains it in depth. Instructional Biology's platform and programs are research-stage and have not completed clinical development.

Instructional Medicine

Instructional Medicine is Instructional Biology's research thesis for a more context-aware approach to biology. It means identifying how biological signals relate to one another, preserving what they mean, and building around what the system is communicating, rather than forcing a single receptor and waiting to see what breaks elsewhere. The company uses the term for its five technology systems run as one integrated program.

Network pharmacology

Network pharmacology is the study of drugs and biology as networks of interacting signals rather than isolated targets. Together with systems biology, it has been an active academic field for well over a decade. Instructional Biology's platform is built to supply the tooling that makes this kind of reasoning workable at the scale a real research program needs.

Read more →

ASAP (Advanced Science AI Platform)

ASAP is Instructional Biology's biology-native AI platform. It organizes biological evidence into the Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph so that relationships between signals, receptors, cascades and outcomes can be reasoned about explicitly instead of inferred from a flat list. It is the discovery stage of the platform: it maps signaling relationships and ranks which questions are worth testing first, with human review at every gate.

Read more →

Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph (SCKG)

The SCKG is a tiered, evidence-weighted map of peptides, receptors, signaling cascades, master regulators, disease states and outcomes, plus the typed, directional relationships between them. It lets a network-level hypothesis be reasoned about explicitly, and each relationship is meant to be traceable back to the evidence that supports it. ASAP is built around it.

Read more →

Poincaré ball

A Poincaré ball is a model of hyperbolic space: a bounded ball in which distance compresses as you move outward, so an entire branching hierarchy fits inside without flattening its structure. Instructional Biology embeds the full Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph in a Poincaré ball of up to 128 dimensions; the Poincaré Explorer page shows a simplified two-dimensional disk projection.

Read more →

Hyperbolic space

Hyperbolic space is a non-Euclidean geometry in which available room grows exponentially as you move away from the center, while flat Euclidean space grows only polynomially. Because a biological hierarchy also branches exponentially, it fits naturally in hyperbolic space without nodes crowding together. Hyperbolic embeddings for hierarchical data were formalized by Nickel and Kiela in 2017.

Read more →

Master regulator

A master regulator is a broadly influential signaling hub that affects many downstream cascades at once. In the Signaling Cascade Knowledge Graph, master regulators sit near the center of the Poincaré disk, while more specific entities such as receptors, peptides and outcomes sit further out. A handful of hub regulators, illustratively the AKT/mTOR pattern, end up with disproportionately more pathway connections than the rest.

Read more →

Graph neural network (GNN)

A graph neural network is a machine-learning model that learns by passing messages along the connections of a graph. Instructional Biology's reasoning system uses GNN message-passing that tracks relationship polarity, so an activating relationship and an inhibiting one between the same two entities are never collapsed into a single signal. Its output is a ranked set of evidence-linked hypotheses, reviewed by a human.

Read more →

Agonist and antagonist

An agonist activates a receptor or pathway; an antagonist blocks or suppresses it. Telling the two apart is a core design choice in Instructional Biology's reasoning system. Modeling inhibition as a magnitude-preserving rotation keeps a strong inhibitory signal distinguishable from no relationship at all.

Read more →

In silico

In silico, literally "in silicon", means running a first pass of testing inside a computational model instead of a physical experiment. It does not replace the lab; it filters a large space of plausible ideas down to the handful worth spending real material and time on. This discipline is at the core of ASAP.

Read more →

MHFBP (Markosian High-Fidelity Bio-Processing Protocol)

MHFBP is Instructional Biology's controlled, lower-temperature processing protocol. It uses carefully regulated temperatures to protect the structure of fragile proteins and peptides, and relies on non-thermal methods rather than heat to manage pathogen safety. The patent filing describes automated temperature controls, monitoring, structural integrity sensing, rapid cooling and defined process limits.

Read more →

Thermosensitive Peptidome (TSP)

The Thermosensitive Peptidome, or TSP, is Instructional Biology's term for the collection of temperature-sensitive signaling peptides and proteins it seeks to protect during processing. These molecules carry their function in their three-dimensional fold, which heat can undo even without breaking a chemical bond. The site describes the band most at risk as roughly a few thousand to around a hundred thousand daltons.

Read more →

Native state

A protein's native state is its working three-dimensional shape, the fold its amino acid sequence guides it into. That shape is what lets an enzyme grip its target or a hormone fit its receptor. Preserving native structure through processing is the goal of Instructional Biology's bio-processing work.

Read more →

Protein denaturation

Denaturation is the loss of a protein's native three-dimensional shape, caused by heat, extreme pH, mechanical shear or other stresses. A denatured protein can still be chemically present but no longer able to do its job, and unfolded proteins tend to clump together, a process called aggregation. A cooked egg white turning solid is a familiar example.

Read more →

Peptide

Peptides and proteins are both chains of amino acids; peptides are the shorter chains, and proteins are longer ones that fold into compact, specific structures. Many of the body's signaling molecules are peptides. Many of the temperature-sensitive signaling molecules Instructional Biology seeks to protect are peptides.

Read more →

Biologic

A biologic is a medicine made from proteins, such as antibodies, hormones and enzymes, rather than a small synthetic chemical. Because biologics depend on a precise shape, their structural sensitivity shapes how they are made, stored and given. Instructional Biology's platform is built on natural, native biologics.

Read more →

Specific-pathogen-free sourcing

Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) sourcing means starting from source material raised or collected to be free of defined pathogens. It reduces the bioburden a process has to guard against in the first place. In MHFBP, that changes how much thermal or chemical force is actually needed to reach an acceptable safety margin.

Read more →

Pathogen reduction

Pathogen reduction is any step that lowers the load of viruses and other pathogens in biological material so it is safe to use. The traditional method is heat, which also damages fragile proteins. Instructional Biology's pathogen-reduction system is non-thermal: ultrashort-pulse optical clearance tuned below the threshold that damages protein structure.

Read more →

ISRS (impulsive stimulated Raman scattering)

ISRS is a physical process in which an extremely short laser pulse drives large vibrations in a molecule. Tuned to a viral capsid's own vibrational mode, it can weaken the bonds holding the capsid together without heating the surrounding material. It was first shown to inactivate viruses in 2007, and it is the published basis for Instructional Biology's pathogen-reduction system, listed on the site as ISRS-ML.

Read more →

Exosome (extracellular vesicle)

Exosomes are nanoscale, membrane-wrapped particles released by cells, roughly 40 to 160 nanometres across, that carry proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and metabolites. They belong to the larger family of extracellular vesicles (EVs), which cells use to exchange molecules. Researchers study them as natural drug-delivery carriers.

Read more →

Exosome carrier (MET-APFS)

Instructional Biology's carrier (exosome) manufacturing system, listed on the site as MET-APFS, builds engineered delivery vesicles modeled on the body's own extracellular vesicle transport. The carrier protects a payload in circulation, and its configuration is informed by the reasoning system for each individual. It is designed to be payload-agnostic, potentially including proteins, peptides and nucleic acid-based cargo.

Read more →

Digital twin

In medicine, a digital twin is a computational model of one person's biological signaling, built from their own data, that updates as new signals arrive. It is not a duplicate of a person or a crystal ball; it is a model bounded by the evidence that built it. For Instructional Biology it is a multi-year research direction, not a current product.

Read more →

Digital biomarker

A biomarker is a measured characteristic that indicates normal biology, disease, or a response to treatment. A digital biomarker is the same idea collected with sensors and software, often outside the clinic through wearable, implantable or ingestible devices. Continuous glucose monitors and smartwatch readings are common sources.

Read more →

Closed-loop dosing

Closed-loop dosing revises a treatment plan based on measured individual response instead of leaving a fixed dose in place. In Instructional Biology's therapeutic-regulation system, the loop closes only within boundaries a clinician has set in advance, the clinician-authorized envelope. Any signal outside that envelope escalates to a human reviewer; it is never an autonomous device decision.

Read more →

Patent cliff

A patent cliff is a period when many branded drugs lose patent exclusivity at once, opening them to generic competition. Between 2025 and 2030, close to 200 branded drugs are set to lose exclusivity, which Instructional Biology calls Patent Cliff 2.0. Its thesis is that the durable opportunity is a reusable way of reasoning about biological signaling, which does not expire the way a single-molecule patent does.
